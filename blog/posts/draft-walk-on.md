Although the spectre of Bing Crosby than haunts Stockholm will have to [keep on
dreaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Christmas_(song)) for another
year, we did get a lovely snowfall on New Year's Eve. Growing up in the
mountains of western Virginia (**not** West Virginia, as I'm frequently at pains
to emphasise), winter meant snow, and often quite a lot of it. Snow of course
meant gallavanting, a favourite childhood pasttime. My fondness for gallavanting
probably explains my enduring fondness for winter, which has made me immune to
the soul-crushing melancholy that Sweden's long dark winters inflict on many.

Until this year.

We didn't get any snow to speak of until this past Tuesday night, meaning the
short dark days of December had nothing to lighten them, and I felt that
darkness pressing on me for the first time in my 14 years in the frozen
northlands.

Walking outside has always improved my mood, but I had been doing a lot less of
that since the beginning of November, when my dog Rover finally succumbed to
chronic kidney disease. He was 11 years old, which is a pretty normal lifespan
for a big dog (Rover was a "yellow dog", mostly Labrador Retriever), and he'd
lived with us for 7 of those years. I'm sure he had a happy life, and it was his
time, but such thoughts are cold solace on cold and dreary "days" where the sun
sets shortly after two in the afternoon.

Working from home (as I've done fulltime since October 2022) means that you have
to make an effort to get out of the house. Having a dog means you have to get
out of the house at least 3-4 times a day, and having a dog that needs lots of
exercise like a Lab (mix) means that you get lots of exercise as well. And
exercise, if you weren't already aware, is fairly [beneficial to one's
health](https://nutritionfacts.org/?s=exercise), increasing one's resistance to
disease, improving cognitive function, regulating blood sugar, etc. Exercise is
also a habit, and my habit of exercising was centred around Rover.

So losing him was what is referred to in the field of psychology a "double
whammy": not only did I miss my best friend and favourite co-worker, but I was
also getting less fresh air and exercise. Thus my descent into the traditional
winter [svårmod](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sv%C3%A5rmod).

I had been pushing myself to get outside and walk, to get those 10,000 steps a
day, but those steps that stacked up so easily when walking alongside Rover felt
so elusive when I was just trudging through the gloom. Having a destination in
mind helped, so I could do things like walking to a store farther away to do the
grocery shopping and so forth. But even then, walking felt more like a thing
that I had to do rather than something I was enjoying.

Until the snow.

Seeing the snow-covered ground at night reminded me of when Rover moved in with
us, back in November of 2017. He was only 4 and full of energy, and then two of
us used to take hour-long walks in the evenings, just exploring the twisty
streets in our neighbourhood and delighting upon discovering a new bike path
that took us some unknown place, often through a small wood. We'd lived in this
place for around 5 years when Rover joined us, but I had visited only a small
fraction of the places Rover and I went during those first couple years of our
walks. Over time, we settled into a pattern of a five or so favourite routes,
choosing which one to take based on how much time we had, the weather, our mood,
etc, so the amount of exploring new places tailed off dramatically. Especially
in the last couple of years, when his age and arthritis meant he couldn't do
more than 3-ish kilometres at a time.
